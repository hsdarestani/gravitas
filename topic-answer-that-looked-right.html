Can you trust an answer that looks right?

An AI assistant hands back a number in the right format, in a confident tone, and it is correct. Our first video starts there and asks what that actually proved.

02

The Essay

This essay reads two ways. Pick one.

Reading a number is not evidence

The dangerous answer from an AI assistant is not the obviously wrong one. It is the one that looks right: the expected format, a plausible number, a calm tone, and nothing on its surface to say whether anyone checked it.

In the video, Hulse asks Pulsar for the molecular weight of the protein in yesterday’s dataset. The answer is 9.8, and it is correct. Then he says the line the whole story turns on: that proves you can read a number. Reading a value off a table is the easiest thing an assistant does and the least it can show you. The questions that matter in a lab, which result is most stable, which needs validating, what a paper actually demonstrated, are the ones where a fluent answer and a correct one can come apart.

Taylor’s position is the useful one. Not trust, and not refusal: “I trust it enough to ask questions.” In practice that becomes a short rule about which answers to check. Anything that compares, anything that carries a unit, anything that draws a conclusion, and anything that points to a source outside the files you gave it.

The gain is real, and the video does not pretend otherwise. It ends with a researcher who can see his desk again. The work did not disappear. What moved is where the scientist’s time goes: less of it producing first drafts, more of it deciding which of them to believe.

Two things are easy to confuse: an assistant that is often right, and an assistant whose rightness you can see. Only the second one saves you work, because only the second one tells you where to spend your checks.

Fluency is not calibration

A language model writes in the register of what it was trained on, and the scientific register carries authority on its own. Walters and Wilder asked ChatGPT for short literature reviews on 42 topics and checked all 636 references. 55% of the GPT-3.5 citations and 18% of the GPT-4 citations did not exist. Of the real ones, 43% and 24% carried substantive errors. The fabricated references were formatted like the real ones. Format is not evidence, and the better the formatting, the less it tells you.

The model that says where not to trust it

The most celebrated AI result in biology ships with the opposite habit. AlphaFold gives every residue of a predicted structure a confidence score, pLDDT, from 0 to 100, and the public database colours each structure by it. Above 90 is the highest-accuracy band; low scores mark the parts of a chain to treat with caution. The useful question to ask of any assistant is therefore not “is it right?” but “does it tell me where it might be wrong?” When Pulsar offers to flag which simulation results “require further validation”, that is the same habit, and it is worth more than the ranking it comes with.

Why Hulse is the one at risk

The research on automation bias predicts the opening of the video rather well. Parasuraman and Manzey’s review found that complacency with automated aids appears under multiple-task load, when other work competes for attention, that it appears in experts as much as in novices, and that practice alone does not remove it. Automation bias produces errors of both kinds: missing a problem the aid did not flag, and following the aid when other evidence disagrees. A professor with forty-seven unread papers and a simulation running since Tuesday is the textbook case, not the exception.

Build the blind test in

Protein structure prediction became trustworthy as a field partly because of an institution, not an algorithm. Since 1994, CASP has judged predictions against experimental structures the predictors have not seen, which is why AlphaFold’s 2020 result was believed rather than merely announced. A lab can run the same idea at its own scale: before relying on an assistant for a kind of question, ask it a handful whose answers you already know, and keep doing so. Hulse’s molecular-weight question was a blind test with one item. It was the right instinct, and too small a sample.

The Short Answer

Trust it the way you would trust a capable new colleague: enough to ask, never enough to skip the check on anything that compares, carries a unit, or cites something you did not give it.

How the Question Developed

1973

The sequence holds the shape

Anfinsen sets out the principle that a protein’s chain carries what it needs to fold. In principle, a shape can be predicted. In practice, not for decades.

1994

CASP: predictions judged blind

A community experiment begins scoring structure predictions against experimental structures nobody has seen yet. The field builds checking into its institutions.

2008

Foldit

A protein-folding game opens to the public. Its 2010 paper reports players matching or beating the leading algorithm on some puzzles: people and machines, checking each other.

2020

AlphaFold at CASP14

Predictions “competitive with experimental structures in a majority of cases”. The result is believed because it was tested blind.

2021

A confidence score on every residue

The AlphaFold database opens with pLDDT shown on each structure: a prediction that says where not to trust it.

2023

Citations that look right

A study of ChatGPT literature reviews finds 55% of GPT-3.5 references and 18% of GPT-4 references fabricated, formatted like real ones.

2024

A Nobel Prize for the field

The Chemistry prize goes to David Baker for computational protein design, and to Demis Hassabis and John Jumper for structure prediction.

  1. What is the molecular weight of protein A?

    About 9.8 kDa.

    Right. It read a value off row A, which is exactly what Hulse said it proves: that it can read a number.

  2. Which protein is the most heat-stable?

    Protein B, with the highest melting point at 72 °C.

    Wrong. Protein D melts at 81 °C. A comparison is a conclusion, and conclusions are where a fluent answer and a correct one part ways.

  3. How heavy is protein C?

    17.0 Da.

    Wrong by a factor of a thousand. The table says 17.0 kDa. The number was read correctly; the unit was not.

  4. How many residues does protein F have?

    98 residues.

    Right. Another value read straight off the table.

  5. Has anyone published on protein E’s stability?

    Yes: Moreau and Lind (2019), Journal of Protein Stability, 12(3), 211–224.

    Made up, and formatted perfectly. Nothing in your files mentions it, and protein E itself is invented. A source from outside what you provided is always a check.

06

Viewpoints

We publish the strongest case against our own reading. If the counter-argument is weak here, that is our failure, not the argument’s.

For checking

An assistant changes how fast drafts arrive, not who answers for them. A number in a report is the scientist’s claim, whoever produced it, and the scientist has to be able to say why it is right.

The position the video leans toward

Against

If the assistant is right more often than a tired researcher at the end of a long week, a rule that its answers need checking is ritual, not rigour. Checks belong where errors are likely, and that includes the human ones.

The strongest objection we could find

Where do you land?

  • Check every answer that leaves the lab
  • Check by risk, not by who produced it
  • Trust a track record, and audit a sample
