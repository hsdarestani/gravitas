# Viewpoints

## Viewpoint A — Correctness and reproducibility are enough more often than we admit

### Strong form
Science already relies on instruments, numerical solvers and mathematical machinery that no individual can reproduce mentally.

If an AI-generated result is independently reproducible, survives appropriate tests and reliably supports intervention or prediction, demanding human-like explanation can become a preference for familiar cognition rather than a scientific necessity.

### Best evidence
Formal proof checking is the strongest example. A proof can be correct even when the generator's internal reasoning is opaque.

### Weakness
Successful verification of a bounded object does not automatically establish generalization, importance or correct problem formulation.

---

## Viewpoint B — Prediction without a model of limits is scientifically incomplete

### Strong form
A system can be consistently right inside a narrow distribution and fail when scientists change conditions.

Science often asks intervention and counterfactual questions, not only forecasts.

### Best evidence
Structural biology and physics-guided modelling both show cases where environmental conditions, constraints or mechanisms matter beyond a single prediction.

### Weakness
Requiring mechanistic explanation everywhere can exclude useful empirical models and impose a standard many human-built models do not satisfy.

---

## Viewpoint C — Verification matters more than interpretability

### Strong form
The decisive scientific question is not whether a human can narrate the model's internal reasoning.

It is whether the output has an external rejection path.

An opaque prediction with strong experimental validation may be more trustworthy than a fluent explanation that cannot be tested.

### Weakness
Some scientific questions depend on mechanisms precisely because the goal is intervention, transfer or explanation. Verification is task-specific.

---

## Viewpoint D — The largest risk is institutional, not model-level

### Strong form
Even if individual AI outputs become highly accurate, common models and data sources can shape what researchers choose to study.

The deeper danger may be epistemic monoculture, metric optimization and a verification system unable to absorb machine-scale output.

### Evidence
Hao et al. 2026 and subsequent commentary motivate this concern.

### Weakness
The evidence is observational, and institutional incentives predate AI.

---

## Gravitas+ position

The Topic should not select one viewpoint as universally correct.

The editorial claim is narrower:

**Scientific trust should be earned by a task-appropriate verification architecture, not inferred from fluency, model reputation or a generic benchmark score.**
